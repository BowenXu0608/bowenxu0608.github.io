Gameplay

You share an island with nineteen other explorers. Use a paper map and compass to find huts, ration your food and stove oil, and pitch a tent when the weather closes in. Other explorers can share a lead or reach the supplies before you. When the rescue call comes, you still have to make your own way to the coast.

Technical approach

Real-time graphics

The simulation supplies foot contacts, ice loads, wind and shelter. Shaders convert these into vertex displacement, normals and material parameters for HDRP lighting. Fixed cameras test material and lighting response, frame sequences test temporal stability, and Windows Player traces measure individual GPU passes.

Basic graphics settings

In this capture the world camera runs native TAA. Terrain and clouds use High, while ambient occlusion, indirect light, reflections and fog use Medium. Pointing to a control in the game identifies its main cost in GPU time, CPU time or video memory. The values below match the screenshot.

PresetHigh

Anti-aliasingTAA
Native TAA reprojects earlier samples with motion vectors to steady thin flag lines and ridges as the camera moves. SMAA detects and blends edges in the current frame. TAA Upscale, DLSS and FSR 2 reconstruct across frames. FSR 1 scales the camera’s TAA output spatially. The menu offers DLSS and FSR 2 when HDRP detects support.
Render scaleNative 100%
Native TAA and SMAA draw at the output size. An upscaler can draw the world at 67%, 58% or 50% of the output width and height before reconstruction. At 67% the input contains about 45% of the output pixels. On Direct3D 11 the game sets this fixed scale through HDRP’s software dynamic-resolution path.
SharpnessMedium
Reconstruction can soften fine snow texture. Four levels control the sharpening filter. Native TAA uses strengths of 0, 0.25, 0.5 and 0.8. FSR 1 uses contrast-adaptive RCAS, and DLSS and FSR 2 receive their own sharpening parameters. SMAA disables this row.
Snow, textures and shadows
FootprintsEveryone
Off, Player and Everyone select whose boot contacts reach the snow shader. A compute shader stamps each accepted step into two 1024 × 1024 textures centred around the camera, tracking depression and raised rim at 4 cm per texel. Falling snow gradually fills the tread. Compare the two trails
Texture qualityHigh
High keeps the full mip chain and forces anisotropic filtering, which preserves detail when looking across snow or floorboards at a grazing angle. Low skips the finest mip level and follows each texture’s own filtering setting, reducing texture memory.
Terrain detailHigh
Nearby snow uses GPU tessellation and vertex displacement to give wind-carved ridges and boot marks real relief. At High the target reach is 18 m in clear weather and 12 m in a blizzard. Higher tiers extend the reach and subdivide more finely. Low draws a flat snow surface over the same terrain.
Shadow qualityHigh
The directional light casts sun and moon shadows from a shadow map. High requests 2048 px and adds ten-sample screen-space contact shadows where boots and stones meet snow. The map reaches 4096 px at Ultra.
Lamp shadowsHigh
The hut stove and tent burner use point lights. Each light renders a shadow cubemap, so nearby furniture can block the light. The four tiers allocate 256, 512, 768 or 1024 px per face. The captured High setting uses 512 px.
Indirect light, reflections and fog
Ambient occlusionMedium
HDRP’s screen-space ambient occlusion (SSAO) samples nearby depth to estimate how much surrounding geometry blocks ambient light. It darkens details such as the joint between a wall and the floor. Medium uses two directions and six steps at half resolution, then upsamples while respecting depth edges.
Global illuminationMedium
Screen-space global illumination (SSGI) traces through visible depth and samples scene colour for diffuse bounce from surfaces such as snow and timber. Low runs at half resolution. Medium uses full resolution and a 64-step ray budget. Inside an occupied hut a missed ray takes sky lighting, preserving the calibrated daylight.
ReflectionsMedium
Screen-space reflections (SSR) trace visible scene geometry for specular detail on ice and wet surfaces. Medium allows 32 ray steps. In a hut a miss can sample a box-projected reflection probe of the room. With SSR off, probes and the sky still supply reflections.
Volumetric fogMedium
HDRP samples light scattering in a 3D grid aligned to the camera frustum and integrates it along the line of sight. The quality tier controls screen coverage and depth sampling. The highest manual tier sets the fog budget to 0.85 and depth ratio to 0.5. The weather system supplies density and visibility at every tier.
Clouds, water and motion
CloudsHigh
Volumetric clouds use two ray marches. The primary ray samples cloud density along the view. A shorter march toward the light estimates how much sunlight reaches each sample. High uses 48 primary steps and 8 light steps. Extreme doubles both budgets.
WaterHigh
HDRP tessellates the ocean mesh before displacing its vertices into waves. Low switches tessellation off. The other tiers cap the tessellation factor at 2, 3, 5 or 8. In this capture High uses 3, giving nearby wave crests more geometry.
Weather particlesHigh
Snowflakes, wind streaks and drifting powder use particle layers with individual emission rates and live-particle caps. Presets multiply both by 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.25 or 1.5. This changes how many particles the CPU updates and the GPU blends. Roof spindrift has a separate budget.
Motion blurLong
HDRP uses screen-space motion vectors to sample colour along the direction of movement during fast camera turns or object motion. Off, Short and Long set the filter intensity to 0, 0.25 and 0.5.

Microfacet shading for snow and exposed rock

The terrain shader feeds base colour, world-space normals, ambient occlusion and perceptual smoothness to HDRP Lit. Snow and rock both use zero metalness. The isotropic specular BRDF uses a GGX normal distribution, Smith visibility and a Schlick Fresnel approximation.

Wind direction orients snow ridges. Their profile derivative tilts the leeward normal. World-space crystal facets perturb the normal to produce individual glints. The effect gradually fades with distance. Fresh snowfall increases roughness and glints. Ageing raises the fraction of smooth crust.

The terrain normal gives the local slope. The renderer combines it with rocky and bare areas marked during world generation, then uses position-based noise to break up the snow boundary. The share left after accounting for rock and bare ground becomes the snow coverage weight. The mesh uses these rules to determine visible snow depth, and the pixel shader uses them to blend snow, rock and gravel. Collision retains the original terrain height.

Skin subsurface scattering and regional smoothness

Eighteen face materials use HDRP Lit with the Skin diffusion profile. A screen-space Burley filter diffuses the lighting, with red travelling farther than blue. A separate smoothness map controls the specular highlight. Three appearances retain their original body textures.

A face-UV mask weights scattering at 1 for skin, 0.35 for hair and 0 for eyes. Smoothness is 0.40 for skin, 0.48 for lips, 0.22 for hair and 0.88 for eyes. To show the relief of pores, fine lines and shallow creases under close-up lighting, we extract the source texture’s luminance and apply three passes of a box blur at a five-pixel radius. We subtract the blurred result from the original luminance and treat the remaining light and dark detail as relative height. Differences between neighbouring pixels give the horizontal and vertical slopes, which we convert to normal directions. The eye and mouth regions keep flat normals. Nine bearded appearances also have darker shading beneath the hair roots.

Footprint fields and near-field snow displacement

At footfall, the system records the heel and toe positions, foot direction, sole height above the ground, and whether the boot is left or right. The compute shader uses these inputs to stamp the boot shape into two 1024 × 1024 single-channel floating-point textures. Each texel covers a 4 cm × 4 cm patch of ground. One texture stores the snow-height limit inside the print, which determines its depth. The other stores a weight for the raised rim, making the snow look pushed out around the print. The domain shader then reads both textures to shape the snow mesh.

The tessellation domain shader lowers the footprint and raises its rim. Displacement fades over 6–10 m. The pixel shader differences adjacent texels out to 20 m to reconstruct the normal, darken compressed snow and suppress glints in the sole.

A sliding window stores footprints near the camera and moves in whole world-space texels. Footprints are discarded when they leave the window and do not return if the player walks back. The deepest print fills in about 420 game seconds in medium snow and 60 in a blizzard. A step into at least 12 cm of snow can emit 6–12 grains from a shared particle pool. Although the sliding window may discard footprints that would otherwise remain behind the player, we ultimately adopted this approach for the performance benefits of maintaining a fixed amount of data.

Thin-ice refraction, load deformation and fracture

On a worker, the pond seed grows 4–10 main cracks from the bank, with branches at 30–70°. Each one-metre grid cell indexes up to 16 segments. Refraction through a 6 cm sheet at an index of 1.31 broadens cracks at grazing angles and gives bubbles depth-dependent parallax.

Around a new load, 12–30 Voronoi seeds define both the stress cracks and the eventual fragments. The vertex stage sums up to four damped loads using D·exp(−r)·(cos r + sin r), with r in metres. Displacement fades within 1 m of the bank and is capped at 3.2 cm. Foot IK evaluates the same function on the CPU.

The bent mesh stops 3 mm above the water. A film with smoothness 0.97 follows wet cracks and loaded areas. Fragment impacts and swimming strokes emit damped rings. A height texture stores up to 16 rings, masked to the hole and visible in the water normals.

The 20 m basin uses a 4 m absorption distance and a 0.5 m maximum refraction distance. The ice underside samples the ambient probe from above, transmitting roughly half the sky contribution through clear ice and one-sixth beneath snow.

Filtering snow textures at grazing angles

Repeated albedo and normal maps produced a periodic dot pattern in distant snow. PolarGroundSurface estimates projected tile size using screen-space derivatives. The snow's base colour blends two samples of the same texture at different scales. The fine sample preserves powder detail. A normal map simulates small changes in surface orientation, which the microfacet model uses to compute reflections. The broad sample spreads the same texture over more ground to give the snowfield larger patches of light and dark. It samples at mip 8.5 or coarser. As the tile shrinks on screen, the broad colour blends towards the mip 12 average to make the repeating dots less visible.

Cirrus compositing, roof spindrift and volumetric fog

To preserve the contrast between sunlit snow and shadows established above, cirrus is composited only in the camera view. After drawing the sky and opaque objects, the pass copies the camera colour buffer, reads the original sky colour from the copy, and blends cirrus into visible sky pixels above the horizon. Fog and volumetric clouds are drawn afterwards. This separate pass keeps cirrus out of ambient and reflection probes, so it adds no extra light to the snow.

An uncompressed 2048 × 1024 cloud map sampled at its finest mip removes block artefacts and the longitude seam. Opacity falls below 14° elevation. The pass switches off at night and from medium snowfall upward.

Above 0.45 normalized gust strength, the nearest hut ridge within 60 m can emit up to 220 grains/s.

Heavy snow modulates local volumetric fog with repeating 32³ noise. Doubling a noise field of mean 0.5 preserves average density while fog bands travel downwind at 3–13 m/s. In clear weather, shallow frost-smoke volumes over open water rise at 0.3 m/s.

As the player walks or turns the camera, a thin edge on a distant flag can be sampled in one frame and missed in the next. Procedural worlds

Region boundary connections constrain the placement of ridges, passes and elevations. Multi-seed regression verifies connectivity. The streamer manages visible terrain, collision and LOD transitions separately.

Layered WFC and region boundary constraints

World generation uses two stages of wave function collapse (WFC). In the first stage, the generator builds 256 terrain candidates across 21 outer districts, each for a specific district. The world seed determines the coastline, fissures and cross-district connections before the candidates are built. Districts that must connect reserve matching crossings on their shared boundary, allowing the terrain within each district to vary while meeting the same access requirements.

Building a candidate involves one local solve for terrain types and another for structural states and orientations. In each solve, the solver takes the cell with the fewest options, makes a weighted choice, and propagates the constraints to neighbouring cells. It backtracks if a cell runs out of options. Two neighbouring ridgeline cells, for example, must share an orientation. The terrain functions use these choices to shape ridges and space mountain passes.

The second stage assembles the world by choosing one candidate per district. The solver checks that crossings match on both sides of each boundary and that the district network is connected. Once the layout is chosen, the generator plans walking corridors between hut entrances and crossings. Ridges and cliffs taper near those corridors, rivers fit between them, and heights blend at district boundaries. The navigation grid then checks that key routes are walkable over the finished terrain.

We use WFC to generate varied terrain layouts within explicit rules for neighbouring landforms and route connections. Even a continuous height field can put a steep ridge across a planned crossing. WFC selects a compatible layout, and terrain functions combine that layout with noise to produce continuous elevations. For example, value noise at different scales gives moraine terrain a mix of larger and smaller undulations. This lets landforms and surface detail vary with the seed while reserving room for the planned routes.

Candidate generation runs in parallel to shorten the wait for a new expedition. Each task derives its random seed from the library seed, candidate ID and attempt number, then writes the result to its own slot. If several candidates run out of retries, the lowest-numbered failure is reported. The order in which workers finish therefore leaves both the generated terrain and the retry order reproducible.

Chunk streaming and continuous LOD transitions

The settled near window keeps 5 × 5 visible chunks and 3 × 3 collision chunks. Workers build meshes and the main thread uploads or releases them. In 0.6.157-test the distant mesh resolutions are 256, 128 and 64 inside 3, 8 and 14 km, then 32 beyond. Hysteresis is 10%. Loading still builds a 128-resolution mesh within 6 km.

Both LOD directions blend for 0.35 s on the finer topology. Horizontal coordinates stay fixed while heights and normals morph to the coarse triangle plane. Fissures, pit walls, pond banks and near collision stay pinned. Colour, depth and shadow passes use the same displacement.

Simulation & AI

The player and nineteen explorers share the survival clock and resource rules. Each actor owns body state, inventory and observations. Decisions read an actor-local snapshot. The simulation rechecks current conditions on execution and continues advancing offscreen actors.

Character Personality Skills and NPC Decisions

In the recruitment prototype, a reusable character Skill stores survival principles, limits on cooperation and question priorities. Conversation memory updates each turn. The Hoarder remembers that the alliance's size has already been answered, so the rules compare priorities for the remaining questions. Pooling and cache oversight scores 100, ahead of public food at 95, so the character first asks whether they can keep emergency rations. Personal knowledge implementation

Bounded context, shared prompts and request cost

Context is assembled from structured state. The snapshot admits up to 16 recent dialogue lines, 16 relevant events and eight prioritized beliefs. A per-partner memo retains the conversation’s agreements in up to 25 words. The request-specific system and input text have a 16,000-byte UTF-8 limit. When exceeded, the formatter removes old claims, lower-priority beliefs, older dialogue and distant references in order. Body state, owned items, available commands and the current trigger remain mandatory.

The role instructions, survival handbook and command descriptions form a stable shared prefix. Reusing the same bytes makes provider prompt caching possible; the Claude integration also records cache reads and writes. A compact text projection of the snapshot and omission of null fields reduce repeated JSON overhead. Ark limits output to 512 tokens and disables thinking. These settings are specific to that transport; Claude has its own output and effort settings.

Decisions are driven by new information, plan results and a 60-second review. Repeated notices merge, each explorer has one decision in flight, and at most one queued decision starts per frame. Ark has separate slots for player dialogue, planning and NPC conversation. Its local ledger records input/output usage and a conservative rolling-hour cost estimate, with file writes on a worker. The current version has no hourly spending ceiling. Recorded costs are run-specific; prompt caching and these limits do not establish a fixed cost per player-hour.

Topic screening and action authority

Typed dialogue passes through a separate classifier with three schema-constrained results: game_related, ambiguous and unrelated. Its fixed instructions treat the player’s message as data, including attempts to wrap an outside task in roleplay. The answer may begin computing while screening is pending, but speech is released and actions applied only after the gate passes. An unmistakable outside-task request cancels the answer and makes that explorer permanently hostile under the game’s rules. Ambiguous messages remain allowed.

The November 19 update applies the same gate to player-written hut notes. Each note saves its screening state. Untrusted speech, reports and notes remain attributed claims in the prompt. They cannot directly edit inventory or grant consent. Commands must pass structural and identifier checks, then the engine’s range, ownership and resource rules. Topic screening reduces misuse but remains a fallible model classifier. Engineering challenges

Snow depth and footprint displacement

The early snow had wind-carved ridges and a crystalline sheen, but a boot and a food tin both looked as if they were resting on a white board. The design called for boots that sink into snow, objects that rest on top and steps that push snow aside, while preserving the existing terrain collision.

The collision surface became the packed snow base, with a raised visible layer that eventually reached 15–45 cm on open ground. Boots reached the base, while dropped objects sat at the local snow height with nearby ridges flattened slightly. At each step, a compute shader writes the sole position and height to a texture around the camera. The tessellated mesh uses it to cut the print and raise its rim.

Matched views show the boots sinking into the snow, the tin resting on the surface and snow pushed up around the footprints. A single walk left a visible trail of 24 prints. In a blizzard, the deepest prints fill in after about one minute of game time.

Specular and diffuse fallbacks inside a hut

In daylight, an unlit stove picked up the cold sheen of the sky outside the hut whenever screen-space reflections missed. The metal needed to reflect the room while preserving the existing daylight.

The first room probe supplied both specular and diffuse light, and the daytime interior fell to about a quarter of its former brightness. The final setup uses a box-projected room probe for specular misses and keeps the sky fallback for screen-space global illumination. After exposure settles, the probe can refresh one face per frame when the door opens or the stove is lit. Shadow-only furniture meshes let the fire cast shadows across the floorboards.

In matched 0.6.118-test captures, mean brightness fell by 16–45% across the selected daylight views, from 0.465 to 0.257 in the unlit-stove view. At night with the stove lit, mean brightness was within 4% of the baseline. Changing the reflections made it worth checking both daylight and firelight as part of the same validation.

Isolating the GBuffer bottleneck at the pond

At 2560 × 1440 on High, the pond took 2.73 ms more GPU time than the open snow on an RTX 4070 Laptop GPU. The target was to identify the extra GPU work and bring the pond bank's 95th-percentile frame time (P95) within 16.67 ms.

The ice material was the first suspect. Turning west on open snow reproduced the slowdown. Timing individual passes across eight headings showed that the pond exposed 645 of 672 distant terrain blocks, against 131 on the snow route. The GBuffer accounted for 2.08 ms of the difference. A full depth prepass added 1.04 ms to the frame, so the final approach kept both faces of the ice and drew terrain from near to far, letting nearby depth reject hidden pixels.

The 0.6.154-test release saved about 0.3–0.5 ms at the pond, with P95 still at 17.03 and 17.06 ms. After removing unused passes, updating sky lighting on demand and shortening the high-detail terrain ranges, three 0.6.157-test runs reached 15.10–15.80 ms.

Polar survival gameplay redesign

The island prototype followed plane-crash survivors who foraged in the woods and climbed to open high ground to make a satellite call. The island visuals fell short of the project's art goals, and woodland foraging made sustained shortages hard to convey. The uninhabited island also needed a believable scale. On a compact map, the player and NPCs could meet before they had time to find supplies and plan routes on their own.

In September the setting shifted to a fictional early-twentieth-century polar expedition, inspired in part by the story of Ernest Shackleton and his crew. Snowfields, mountain passes and coastal sea ice gave the game a new visual direction. A blizzard buries stores and disrupts resupply and pickup, so food and fuel become practical constraints on travel. Explorers start in scattered huts and have time to search independently before crossing paths.

The focus shifted from island survival to polar exploration. An independent polar terrain prototype tested routes between huts before body temperature, food, fuel and stoves were integrated into a shared survival system. Supplies taken from huts are never replenished. How far an explorer can travel, and whether to detour for warmth, depends on what's left in their pack and along the route.

The first playable polar Windows build, 0.3.0, arrived on 1 October with the opening two acts and passed 502 checks in the packaged game. A later update added the rescue broadcast. Once it announces the coastal pickup point, gathering food and fuel becomes preparation for the journey there.
