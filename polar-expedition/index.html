Gameplay

You share an island with nineteen other explorers. Use a paper map and compass to find huts, ration your food and stove oil, and pitch a tent when the weather closes in. Other explorers can share a lead or reach the supplies before you. When the rescue call comes, you still have to make your own way to the coast.

Technical approach

Real-time graphics

The simulation supplies foot contacts, ice loads, wind and shelter. Shaders convert these into vertex displacement, normals and material parameters for HDRP lighting. Fixed cameras test material and lighting response, frame sequences test temporal stability, and Windows Player traces measure individual GPU passes.

Basic graphics settings

In this capture the world camera runs native TAA. Terrain and clouds use High, while ambient occlusion, indirect light, reflections and fog use Medium. Pointing to a control in the game identifies its main cost in GPU time, CPU time or video memory. The values below match the screenshot.

PresetHigh

Anti-aliasingTAA
Native TAA reprojects earlier samples with motion vectors to steady thin flag lines and ridges as the camera moves. SMAA detects and blends edges in the current frame. TAA Upscale, DLSS and FSR 2 reconstruct across frames. FSR 1 scales the camera’s TAA output spatially. The menu offers DLSS and FSR 2 when HDRP detects support.
Render scaleNative 100%
Native TAA and SMAA draw at the output size. An upscaler can draw the world at 67%, 58% or 50% of the output width and height before reconstruction. At 67% the input contains about 45% of the output pixels. On Direct3D 11 the game sets this fixed scale through HDRP’s software dynamic-resolution path.
SharpnessMedium
Reconstruction can soften fine snow texture. Four levels control the sharpening filter. Native TAA uses strengths of 0, 0.25, 0.5 and 0.8. FSR 1 uses contrast-adaptive RCAS, and DLSS and FSR 2 receive their own sharpening parameters. SMAA disables this row.
Snow, textures and shadows
FootprintsEveryone
Off, Player and Everyone select whose boot contacts reach the snow shader. A compute shader stamps each accepted step into two 1024 × 1024 textures centred around the camera, tracking depression and raised rim at 4 cm per texel. Falling snow gradually fills the tread. Compare the two trails
Texture qualityHigh
High keeps the full mip chain and forces anisotropic filtering, which preserves detail when looking across snow or floorboards at a grazing angle. Low skips the finest mip level and follows each texture’s own filtering setting, reducing texture memory.
Terrain detailHigh
Nearby snow uses GPU tessellation and vertex displacement to give wind-carved ridges and boot marks real relief. At High the target reach is 18 m in clear weather and 12 m in a blizzard. Higher tiers extend the reach and subdivide more finely. Low draws a flat snow surface over the same terrain.
Shadow qualityHigh
The directional light casts sun and moon shadows from a shadow map. High requests 2048 px and adds ten-sample screen-space contact shadows where boots and stones meet snow. The map reaches 4096 px at Ultra.
Lamp shadowsHigh
The hut stove and tent burner use point lights. Each light renders a shadow cubemap, so nearby furniture can block the light. The four tiers allocate 256, 512, 768 or 1024 px per face. The captured High setting uses 512 px.
Indirect light, reflections and fog
Ambient occlusionMedium
HDRP’s screen-space ambient occlusion (SSAO) samples nearby depth to estimate how much surrounding geometry blocks ambient light. It darkens details such as the joint between a wall and the floor. Medium uses two directions and six steps at half resolution, then upsamples while respecting depth edges.
Global illuminationMedium
Screen-space global illumination (SSGI) traces through visible depth and samples scene colour for diffuse bounce from surfaces such as snow and timber. Low runs at half resolution. Medium uses full resolution and a 64-step ray budget. Inside an occupied hut a missed ray takes sky lighting, preserving the calibrated daylight.
ReflectionsMedium
Screen-space reflections (SSR) trace visible scene geometry for specular detail on ice and wet surfaces. Medium allows 32 ray steps. In a hut a miss can sample a box-projected reflection probe of the room. With SSR off, probes and the sky still supply reflections.
Volumetric fogMedium
HDRP samples light scattering in a 3D grid aligned to the camera frustum and integrates it along the line of sight. The quality tier controls screen coverage and depth sampling. The highest manual tier sets the fog budget to 0.85 and depth ratio to 0.5. The weather system supplies density and visibility at every tier.
Clouds, water and motion
CloudsHigh
Volumetric clouds use two ray marches. The primary ray samples cloud density along the view. A shorter march toward the light estimates how much sunlight reaches each sample. High uses 48 primary steps and 8 light steps. Extreme doubles both budgets.
WaterHigh
HDRP tessellates the ocean mesh before displacing its vertices into waves. Low switches tessellation off. The other tiers cap the tessellation factor at 2, 3, 5 or 8. In this capture High uses 3, giving nearby wave crests more geometry.
Weather particlesHigh
Snowflakes, wind streaks and drifting powder use particle layers with individual emission rates and live-particle caps. Presets multiply both by 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.25 or 1.5. This changes how many particles the CPU updates and the GPU blends. Roof spindrift has a separate budget.
Motion blurLong
HDRP uses screen-space motion vectors to sample colour along the direction of movement during fast camera turns or object motion. Off, Short and Long set the filter intensity to 0, 0.25 and 0.5.
Graphics settings implementation

Microfacet shading for snow and exposed rock

The terrain shader feeds base colour, world-space normals, ambient occlusion and perceptual smoothness to HDRP Lit. Snow and rock both use zero metalness. The isotropic specular BRDF uses a GGX normal distribution, Smith visibility and a Schlick Fresnel approximation.

Wind direction orients snow ridges. Their profile derivative tilts the leeward normal. World-space crystal facets perturb the normal to produce individual glints. The effect gradually fades with distance. Fresh snowfall increases roughness and glints. Ageing raises the fraction of smooth crust.

The terrain normal gives the local slope. The renderer combines it with rocky and bare areas marked during world generation, then uses position-based noise to break up the snow boundary. The share left after accounting for rock and bare ground becomes the snow coverage weight. The mesh uses these rules to determine visible snow depth, and the pixel shader uses them to blend snow, rock and gravel. Collision retains the original terrain height.

Terrain shading implementation

Skin subsurface scattering and regional smoothness

Eighteen face materials use HDRP Lit with the Skin diffusion profile. A screen-space Burley filter diffuses the lighting, with red travelling farther than blue. A separate smoothness map controls the specular highlight. Three appearances retain their original body textures.

A face-UV mask weights scattering at 1 for skin, 0.35 for hair and 0 for eyes. Smoothness is 0.40 for skin, 0.48 for lips, 0.22 for hair and 0.88 for eyes. To show the relief of pores, fine lines and shallow creases under close-up lighting, we extract the source texture’s luminance and apply three passes of a box blur at a five-pixel radius. We subtract the blurred result from the original luminance and treat the remaining light and dark detail as relative height. Differences between neighbouring pixels give the horizontal and vertical slopes, which we convert to normal directions. The eye and mouth regions keep flat normals. Nine bearded appearances also have darker shading beneath the hair roots.

Face material implementation

Footprint fields and near-field snow displacement

At footfall, the system records the heel and toe positions, foot direction, sole height above the ground, and whether the boot is left or right. The compute shader uses these inputs to stamp the boot shape into two 1024 × 1024 single-channel floating-point textures. Each texel covers a 4 cm × 4 cm patch of ground. One texture stores the snow-height limit inside the print, which determines its depth. The other stores a weight for the raised rim, making the snow look pushed out around the print. The domain shader then reads both textures to shape the snow mesh.

The tessellation domain shader lowers the footprint and raises its rim. Displacement fades over 6–10 m. The pixel shader differences adjacent texels out to 20 m to reconstruct the normal, darken compressed snow and suppress glints in the sole.

A sliding window stores footprints near the camera and moves in whole world-space texels. Footprints are discarded when they leave the window and do not return if the player walks back. The deepest print fills in about 420 game seconds in medium snow and 60 in a blizzard. A step into at least 12 cm of snow can emit 6–12 grains from a shared particle pool. Although the sliding window may discard footprints that would otherwise remain behind the player, we ultimately adopted this approach for the performance benefits of maintaining a fixed amount of data.

Thin-ice refraction, load deformation and fracture

On a worker, the pond seed grows 4–10 main cracks from the bank, with branches at 30–70°. Each one-metre grid cell indexes up to 16 segments. Refraction through a 6 cm sheet at an index of 1.31 broadens cracks at grazing angles and gives bubbles depth-dependent parallax.

Around a new load, 12–30 Voronoi seeds define both the stress cracks and the eventual fragments. The vertex stage sums up to four damped loads using D·exp(−r)·(cos r + sin r), with r in metres. Displacement fades within 1 m of the bank and is capped at 3.2 cm. Foot IK evaluates the same function on the CPU.

The bent mesh stops 3 mm above the water. A film with smoothness 0.97 follows wet cracks and loaded areas. Fragment impacts and swimming strokes emit damped rings. A height texture stores up to 16 rings, masked to the hole and visible in the water normals.

The 20 m basin uses a 4 m absorption distance and a 0.5 m maximum refraction distance. The ice underside samples the ambient probe from above, transmitting roughly half the sky contribution through clear ice and one-sixth beneath snow.

Filtering snow textures at grazing angles

Repeated albedo and normal maps produced a periodic dot pattern in distant snow. PolarGroundSurface estimates projected tile size using screen-space derivatives. The snow's base colour blends two samples of the same texture at different scales. The fine sample preserves powder detail. A normal map simulates small changes in surface orientation, which the microfacet model uses to compute reflections. The broad sample spreads the same texture over more ground to give the snowfield larger patches of light and dark. It samples at mip 8.5 or coarser. As the tile shrinks on screen, the broad colour blends towards the mip 12 average to make the repeating dots less visible.

Calibrating direct light, skylight and cascaded shadows

At a fixed 14:00 camera, tone mapping and colour grading were disabled. Volumetric cloud densities of 0.45, 0.30, 0.20 and 0.12 yielded direct-sun contributions of 22%, 30%, 37% and 45% on lit snow. Clear daylight uses 0.12 and +0.8 EV exposure, blended at dawn and dusk.

Mean linear snow luminance changed from 0.532 to 0.529 while sun-to-shadow contrast grew from about one-third of a stop to nearly one stop. The HDRP Volume sets four 2048 px directional shadow cascades ending at 25, 75, 150 and 500 m, with a 15% terminal fade.

Cirrus compositing, sheltered spindrift and volumetric fog

Cirrus composites onto far-plane pixels above the horizon after sky and opaques, before fog and volumetric clouds. D3D11 first copies camera colour. Keeping cirrus out of lighting probes avoids the 26–93% extra skylight measured on snow in the original cloud-layer trial.

An uncompressed 2048 × 1024 cloud map sampled at its finest mip removes block artefacts and the longitude seam. Opacity falls below 14° elevation. The pass switches off at night and from medium snowfall upward. At 14:00 mean snow luminance was 0.5700 off and 0.5704/0.5698 on. The extra colour buffer costs about 29 MB at 1440p.

Huts and tents shelter downwind wedges of 24 m and 9 m. Ground-drift opacity drops by up to 95% and travel by up to 80%. Above 0.45 normalized gust strength, the nearest hut ridge within 60 m can emit up to 220 grains/s.

Heavy snow modulates local volumetric fog with repeating 32³ noise. Doubling a noise field of mean 0.5 preserves average density while bands travel downwind at 3–13 m/s. In clear weather, shallow frost-smoke volumes over open water rise at 0.3 m/s.

Indoor reflection probes, exposure and stove shadows

The occupied hut uses a 6.2 × 4.4 × 8.6 m box-projected reflection probe with a 10 cm blend. Metals sample the room cubemap on an SSR miss. Diffuse SSGI misses use the sky. Sampling the dark room cubemap for diffuse misses made the daylight interior about four times darker.

After the camera adapts to indoor exposure, the probe renders one cubemap face per frame. Entry, a door or stove change, each game hour and a quarter-stop exposure shift request a refresh. Shelves, bunk posts, table legs and fallen boards share one shadow-only mesh per stove-light shadow face. Furniture gains local shadows with night-view mean brightness within 4% of baseline.

Native TAA and four upscaling paths

Native-resolution TAA is the default. It reprojects history to stabilise moving cloth and subpixel edges, with sharpening set to 0.5 on Medium. High SMAA blends current-frame edges and gives a crisper still image but more shimmer in motion. It uses no extra sharpening.

HDRP supplies TAA Upscale. FSR 1 spatially upscales camera TAA output and sharpens with RCAS. DLSS and FSR 2 use their own temporal reconstruction. Quality, Balanced and Performance render at 67%, 58% and 50% per axis. At 67% the scene has about 45% of native pixels. Unavailable saved modes fall back to native TAA.

0.6.123-test on an RTX 4070 Laptop, 1600 × 900 foreground, v-sync and frame cap off. After 30 warm-up frames, 60 frames were sampled. Mean whole-frame GPU time was 7.72/7.74 ms for native TAA/SMAA and 5.69–6.16 ms for the four 67% modes.

Procedural worlds

Region boundary connections constrain the placement of ridges, passes and elevations. Multi-seed regression verifies connectivity. The streamer manages visible terrain, collision and LOD transitions separately.

Layered WFC and region boundary constraints

The early island prototype plans the coastline and drainage, then uses wave function collapse (WFC) to assemble terrain from 128 tile candidates. Each grid cell starts with a set of options. The solver first picks the cell with the lowest weighted entropy, where the remaining options and their weights leave the least uncertainty. Placing a tile rules out incompatible edges in neighbouring cells, and those restrictions propagate outwards. If a cell runs out of options, the solver backtracks and tries another tile. Tiles can be rotated or mirrored.

Polar world v9 plans which districts must connect, then picks terrain for 21 districts from 256 candidate templates. Wherever two districts need a route between them, their entrances must line up along the shared boundary. Different seeds can assemble different terrain while preserving the planned connections.

A second WFC pass inside each district chooses among six structural states and four orientations. For example, neighbouring cells that both form a ridgeline must agree on its orientation. These choices steer ridges and determine the distribution of mountain passes, while heights blend at district boundaries. Across 20 seeds, the median pairwise difference in large-scale terrain tiles was 82.5% after rotational alignment.

World v15 builds candidate templates on multiple threads. Each candidate derives its own random seed from the template library seed, its candidate ID and its attempt number, and writes the result back into its original slot. If several candidates exhaust their retries, the lowest-numbered failure is reported, matching serial order. All 20 baseline seeds retained their world hashes after parallelisation.

Chunk streaming and continuous LOD transitions

The settled near window keeps 5 × 5 visible chunks and 3 × 3 collision chunks. Workers build meshes and the main thread uploads or releases them. In 0.6.157-test the distant mesh resolutions are 256, 128 and 64 inside 3, 8 and 14 km, then 32 beyond. Hysteresis is 10%. Loading still builds a 128-resolution mesh within 6 km.

Both LOD directions blend for 0.35 s on the finer topology. Horizontal coordinates stay fixed while heights and normals morph to the coarse triangle plane. Fissures, pit walls, pond banks and near collision stay pinned. Colour, depth and shadow passes use the same displacement.

Simulation & AI

The player and nineteen explorers share the survival clock and resource rules. Each actor owns body state, inventory and observations. Decisions read an actor-local snapshot. The simulation rechecks current conditions on execution and continues advancing offscreen actors.

Character Personality Skills and NPC Decisions

In the recruitment prototype, a reusable character Skill stores survival principles, limits on cooperation and question priorities. Conversation memory updates each turn. The Hoarder remembers that the alliance's size has already been answered, so the rules compare priorities for the remaining questions. Pooling and cache oversight scores 100, ahead of public food at 95, so the character first asks whether they can keep emergency rations. A clear answer and its source go into memory, leaving public food for the next turn. A demand to hand over all private rations also weighs against joining according to the character's priorities and the reliability of the source.

The polar game carries forward eight personality archetypes. Each decision gives the model the explorer's tendencies, dated and attributed memories, current body temperature and supplies, and available actions. An explorer who protects their reserves might remember inspecting fuel at a nearby hut and hear that a farther hut has more. If cold and out of fuel, they can favour the shorter journey to the hut they inspected. Finding it empty updates that memory and can send them somewhere else on the next decision.

Personality and decision context in the polar game

Per-actor knowledge snapshots and report provenance

Decision input is limited to the actor’s body, pack, eight nearest known locations, people in view, last-known positions and inspected supplies. Hut inventories retain the last observed count until another inspection or a report updates the actor’s knowledge.

Each report records its subject, original source, latest speaker, event time, hearing time, channel and hop count. Relaying preserves the event time. Nearby awake actors can overhear. Diary entries identify strangers by appearance until learning a name updates the source labels.

Personal knowledge implementation

Bounded context, shared prompts and request cost

Context is assembled from structured state. The snapshot admits up to 16 recent dialogue lines, 16 relevant events and eight prioritized beliefs. A per-partner memo retains the conversation’s agreements in up to 25 words. The request-specific system and input text have a 16,000-byte UTF-8 limit. When exceeded, the formatter removes old claims, lower-priority beliefs, older dialogue and distant references in order. Body state, owned items, available commands and the current trigger remain mandatory.

The role instructions, survival handbook and command descriptions form a stable shared prefix. Reusing the same bytes makes provider prompt caching possible; the Claude integration also records cache reads and writes. A compact text projection of the snapshot and omission of null fields reduce repeated JSON overhead. Ark limits output to 512 tokens and disables thinking. These settings are specific to that transport; Claude has its own output and effort settings.

Decisions are driven by new information, plan results and a 60-second review. Repeated notices merge, each explorer has one decision in flight, and at most one queued decision starts per frame. Ark has separate slots for player dialogue, planning and NPC conversation. Its local ledger records input/output usage and a conservative rolling-hour cost estimate, with file writes on a worker. The current version has no hourly spending ceiling. Recorded costs are run-specific; prompt caching and these limits do not establish a fixed cost per player-hour.

Command validation and physical item transfer

A response carries dialogue and one to four structured commands. The allowed verbs follow actor capabilities. Target and item IDs are checked against the decision snapshot, then validated against the live scene to account for ownership changes while the model was responding.

Physical exchange uses drop, approach and pickup. An explorer senses the four nearest drops within 10 m, walks within 1.5 m and rechecks line of sight, reach, free capacity and ownership. Witnesses record the pickup. Failure reasons feed the next decision.

Topic screening and action authority

Typed dialogue passes through a separate classifier with three schema-constrained results: game_related, ambiguous and unrelated. Its fixed instructions treat the player’s message as data, including attempts to wrap an outside task in roleplay. The answer may begin computing while screening is pending, but speech is released and actions applied only after the gate passes. An unmistakable outside-task request cancels the answer and makes that explorer permanently hostile under the game’s rules. Ambiguous messages remain allowed.

The November 19 update applies the same gate to player-written hut notes. Each note saves its screening state. NPCs can read it only after approval; a rejected note stays on the board for the player, and a failed screening retries after 30 seconds. Screening a note does not change an explorer’s attitude. This closes the alternate path by which instructions could enter through a shared notice board.

Untrusted speech, reports and notes remain attributed claims in the prompt. They cannot directly edit inventory or grant consent. Commands must pass structural and identifier checks, then the engine’s range, ownership and resource rules. Topic screening reduces misuse but remains a fallible model classifier. The current client-side integration still needs a shipping decision on key custody and per-user service limits.

Memory optimization

Keep unused prefabs out of the main menu

Memory snapshots traced 76 unused prefabs to managers that loaded at startup. Registering empty managers first broke the reference chain. On the same 1080p High benchmark route in Development builds, peak process private memory fell from 6.40 to 5.08 GiB.

Optimization results
Unload the world when an expedition ends

HDRP still held references to the previous world after the player quit. Once terrain streaming and the audio fade finish, the game detaches the world from HDRP, releases its textures and unloads unused assets. At the menu between two expeditions in a 1080p High release build, process private memory fell from 4.85 to 3.70 GiB and video memory from 2.00 to 1.04 GB.

Frame-time optimization

Update sky lighting on demand inside huts

With the stove lit, HDRP recalculated sky lighting every frame. The game now updates it on demand, and the hut’s reflection probe requests the lighting it needs as it captures each cubemap face. At a fixed midnight view of the lit hut, the change saved 1.05 ms of GPU time per frame on an RTX 4070 Laptop GPU at 1440p High.

Optimization results
Stagger updates for 19 explorers

All 19 explorers used to update in the same frame. Assigning each explorer one of 20 slots by ID spread the work across frames while preserving one update per game second. In matched 960-second walks with model inference off, the 99th-percentile frame time fell from 21.12 to 16.87 ms. Frames where the game’s own work exceeded 25 ms fell from 181 to 6.

Engineering challenges

Snow depth and footprint displacement

The early snow already had wind-scoured lines and a crystalline sheen. Looking down gave it away. A boot and a food tin both sat on what looked like a board painted white. Colour and normals suggested relief, but neither object had a layer of snow to settle into. We kept the existing collision surface as the packed base and first raised the visible snow by up to 10 cm. A standing explorer’s boots reached that base, leaving the snow partway up the boot. A dropped tin was placed at the local snow height, with nearby ridges flattened enough to keep it visible. Open snow later reached 15–45 cm. At each step a compute shader writes the sole position and height into a texture around the camera. The tessellated mesh takes the impression and raises a rim of displaced snow along its edge. We checked the snowline in matched views of the boots, then followed a trail of 24 prints. In a blizzard the deepest impressions fill in about one minute of game time.

Specular and diffuse fallbacks inside a hut

In daylight an unlit stove picked up the cold sheen of the sky outside its hut. A screen-space reflection ray that missed the geometry in view fell back to the sky. We placed a box-projected reflection probe inside the occupied hut. In an early trial both specular reflections and diffuse bounce sampled the room, and the daylight interior fell to about a quarter of its former brightness. We kept the room probe for specular misses and let screen-space global illumination (SSGI) continue to take sky light on a miss. The probe waits for exposure to settle and refreshes its cubemap one face per frame when the door opens or the stove lights. Shadow-only furniture meshes let the fire cast shadows across the floorboards. In matched captures from 0.6.118-test, mean brightness fell by 16–45% across the selected daytime views, from 0.465 to 0.257 for the unlit stove view. Lighting the stove at night provided a second check. Those views stayed within 4% of their original brightness.

Isolating the GBuffer bottleneck at the pond

At 2560 × 1440 on High, the pond cost 2.73 ms more GPU time than the open snow on an RTX 4070 Laptop GPU. Ice filled the view, so we first suspected its material. Then we stood on open snow, turned west and reproduced the slowdown. Timing each HDRP pass across eight headings showed what the camera brought into view. The pond bank exposed 645 of 672 distant terrain blocks against 131 on the snow route, and the GBuffer pass accounted for 2.08 ms of the difference. A full depth prepass added 1.04 ms to the frame. We kept both faces of the ice and drew terrain near to far so depth from nearby ground could reject hidden pixels. That saved about 0.3–0.5 ms of GPU time at the pond in the 0.6.154-test release build. The two runs still recorded 95th-percentile frame times of 17.03 and 17.06 ms against a 16.67 ms budget. After removing unused passes, updating sky lighting on demand and shortening the high-detail terrain ranges, three 0.6.157-test pond runs measured 15.10–15.80 ms at the 95th percentile.

Thin-ice fracture and water entry

In the early pond a step through weak ice opened a 65 cm pit with vertical walls and glassy shards of ice hanging above the water. We brought the water to 3 cm below the sheet and gave the hole a ragged rim. The still image improved, but the break still looked abrupt in motion. The pieces appeared already arranged in their final floating positions. We started recording the spot where the boot loaded the ice. Cracks now spread along the edges of the same Voronoi cells that form the fragments, so the lines the player sees become the edges that part. Pieces fold into the water over 0.2–0.5 seconds, send out ripples and drift aside when a swimmer pushes past. The body and camera dip together, taking the eye about 35 cm below the surface. The gasp plays as the explorer comes back up. On pond 33, seed 777, we compared the frame 0.15 seconds after the break to check the fragments mid-fall, then watched the whole entry and resurfacing in first person. In 0.6.102-test, the 95th-percentile GPU time during a break was 0.25 ms above the same pond at rest.

Polar survival gameplay redesign

On the original island, survivors of a plane crash could forage in the woods and climb to open high ground to call for help on a satellite phone. Moving the game to an early twentieth-century polar expedition in September meant rethinking both food and rescue. We wanted scarcity to keep testing whom the player could trust. Explorers now set out from separate huts and search stores of food and stove fuel that never refill. A tin taken by the first visitor is gone for the next, which gives a traveller a reason to share a lead or keep a route quiet. We kept the island prototype, tested routes between huts in a separate polar world, then brought body temperature, inventory and stove heat into one survival simulation. On 1 October, the first polar Windows release, 0.3.0, made the opening two acts playable and passed 502 checks in the packaged game. Radio rescue came later. The ship’s first broadcast comes in clear daylight, on day 12 or later. Until that message arrives, a food tin might get an explorer through the night. Afterwards it might take them one hut closer to the coast.
